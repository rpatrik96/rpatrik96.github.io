Steering ≠ encoding

Adding α·v reliably shifts a toy model toward refusal. That licenses a control handle (interventional evidence — “L2”, rung 2 of Pearl’s causal ladder); it does not license “the model represents refusal”, which claims v is an internal causal variable.

Licensed by this evidence

A control handle (L2)

L2 = the intervention rung of Pearl’s ladder (what happens when you act on the system), not the ℓ₂ norm.

Adding a scaled direction α·v to the activations controllably shifts the output distribution toward refusal. That is interventional evidence: v affords behavioural control on this prompt distribution.

Not licensed by this evidence

An internal causal variable

Reading the same result as “the model represents refusal” asserts that v is a causal variable mediating the model’s computation. This toy satisfies all the steering evidence while v is non-unique, entangles politeness, and miscalibrates on novel prompts; the stronger claim needs structural assumptions beyond controllability.

What this shows

Every control manipulates one toy model: seeded prompts embedded as 8-dimensional activations, a linear refusal readout, and a linear politeness readout. The slider adds α·v to every activation and the refusal distribution slides past the boundary, so steering genuinely works, and that success is real interventional (L2) evidence. But the measured behaviour constrains only one coordinate of v: its projection onto the refusal readout. The three vectors agree on that coordinate and differ everywhere else, which makes them interchangeable for the evidence you collected (non-unique), free to move concepts you never measured (entangled), and calibrated only to the prompt distribution you tested (the same α overshoots on novel prompts).

This is the paper’s steering ≠ encoding gap. Describing a successful steering result as “the model represents refusal” admits a stronger reading than the evidence supports, because it asserts that v is an internal causal variable mediating the model’s computation; the paper notes that “steering vectors miscalibrate OOD” (Turner et al., 2023). What would resolve it, per the paper: evidence that v is a stable, reusable causal variable, e.g. transfer across distributions (Todd et al., 2023), specificity to a constrained subspace (Marks et al., 2025), and a causal abstraction in which interventions on v compose appropriately with other model computations (Geiger et al., 2021).

What the paper says

“However, v may entangle multiple concepts, may not be unique, and may exploit distributional shortcuts rather than the model’s natural computation (Turner et al., 2023; Jorgensen et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025; Mueller et al., 2025).”

“Steering identifies a control handle on a particular concept, but claiming that the model represents that concept asserts that it is an internal causal variable. The former is supported by L2 evidence, while the latter requires additional structural assumptions beyond controllability.”

— Causality is Key for Interpretability Claims to Generalise (arXiv:2602.16698), “Steering ≠ Encoding” mismatch box

Try this

  1. Calibrate: with v₁ on in-distribution prompts, find the α that produces roughly 40% refusal, and write the one-sentence claim you would put in the abstract. Now toggle to novel prompts without touching α. Which word in your sentence failed first?
  2. Set α = 1 and cycle v₁ → v₂ → v₃ watching only the refusal curve; then cycle again watching the politeness meter. What exactly did the refusal evidence constrain about v, and what did it leave free?
  3. The paper’s resolution list: transfer across distributions, specificity to a constrained subspace, compositional interventions. Match each test to the symptom chip it would catch in this toy, and decide which test your favourite steering paper actually ran.
