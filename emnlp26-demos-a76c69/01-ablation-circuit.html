Computed live from the seeded toy: F(S) = 1 − MSE(y_S, y_full) / MSE(y_∅, y_full), where y_∅ is the fully-ablated model (every component knocked out). F = 1: the circuit alone reproduces the full model’s output; F = 0: no better than ablating everything. Greedy search until F ≥ 0.93, then pruned to a minimal set. This behavioural faithfulness metric is unrelated to the causal-discovery “faithfulness” assumption.

At the default threshold τ = 0.93:

  • h1.c and m2.b enter only the zero-ablation circuit: an always-on head never outputs 0 on real inputs, so a zero baseline drags it off-distribution and the search must keep it and its gate, even though neither routes task information.
  • h1.d leaves when you move from mean to resample: the reader m2.c only uses the property h1.d² = 1, which any resampled value still satisfies, whereas the dataset mean (≈0) destroys it.
  • h2.d makes it into no circuit: the three searches agree on the exclusions yet disagree on what “the circuit” is.

What this shows

Every run above uses the identical network, dataset, and greedy search; at any fixed threshold, the ablation baseline is the only degree of freedom, and the τ slider exposes the second one. Zero-ablation returns the largest circuit because it must retain a constant head and its gate that carry no task signal. Mean-ablation drops those but must retain a format head whose magnitude a downstream reader uses. Resample-ablation keeps activations on-distribution and returns the leanest set. In causal terms, each baseline fixes a different interventional reference value: setting a knocked-out component to zero, to its mean, or to a resampled value are three different do-interventions (do(·) means forcing a variable to a chosen value instead of letting the model compute it), so each strategy estimates a different estimand (the technical term for the precise quantity an analysis sets out to measure). All three circuits recover the model’s behaviour to the same faithfulness, so behavioural faithfulness alone cannot arbitrate between them: the discovered circuit is a property of the (model, ablation family, threshold) triple, not of the model alone.

This is the paper’s Existence ≠ Uniqueness mismatch in miniature. Finding a subgraph whose ablation changes behaviour establishes that a sufficient circuit exists, and the paper’s ladder-of-claims table — its table mapping each interpretability method to the strongest causal claim it can support — scopes that evidence as “an intervention effect for the chosen ablation, not yet a unique localisation”. The pattern also shows up at scale: Wang et al. (2023) find multiple redundant circuits for the same task in their Indirect Object Identification study, other circuits can implement equivalent input–output behaviour (Wang et al., 2023; McGrath et al., 2023), and a circuit whose ablation suppresses a behaviour on the test set can prove brittle under distribution shift (Miller et al., 2024).

What the paper says

In causal-inference terms: an existence result says some sufficient circuit is out there; an identification result would pin down which one, up to variants that are genuinely interchangeable (an equivalence class, written ∼).

“Showing ∃S is an existence result, not an identification result that establishes uniqueness up to an equivalence class ∼. Multiple subgraphs may produce the same effect through redundant pathways, distributed implementations, or equivalent reparameterisations. The method identifies one member of an equivalence class of sufficient circuits, and which one it returns depends on initialisation, optimisation choices, etc.”

— mismatch box “Existence ≠ Uniqueness”, arXiv:2602.16698

“Without ruling out alternatives, we can only consider the uncovered subgraphs from circuit discovery as intervention handles, and not unique explanations.”

— takeaway of the same mismatch box, arXiv:2602.16698

Try this

  1. Switch from zero to mean and watch h1.c and m2.b leave the circuit while faithfulness stays put. Neither routes any task information; what made them look like circuit components under the zero baseline, and would you have reported them as part of “the” circuit?
  2. Switch from mean to resample: h1.d drops out even though m2.c genuinely reads it. What property of resampling makes h1.d dispensable, and what does that imply for interpreting circuit membership as “necessary for the behaviour”?
  3. Drag τ down and watch every circuit shrink toward the core signal path (below 0.93, mean- and resample-ablation even agree exactly); drag it up to 0.99 and watch them grow and diverge, with the zero-ablation circuit growing the most. If circuit membership depends on how faithful is “faithful enough”, which threshold would you defend in a paper, and why?
  4. The paper’s claims checklist asks authors to test robustness by varying, among other things, “ablation strategies (zero/mean/resample)”. Take a circuit paper you know: which of its claims would survive if the reported circuit changed under a different baseline, and which would not?
