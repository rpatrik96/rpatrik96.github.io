Three Rungs, Three Worked Examples

A sentiment probe, an activation patch, and a true counterfactual: three diagnostic questions locate each piece of evidence on Pearl’s causal ladder—and show exactly what it licenses.

Do this first: pick a rung below—the three cards are clickable tabs—then run its experiment: the dimmed verdict cards unlock when you do. Each rung is one level of causal evidence: L1 observes correlations, L2 intervenes (Pearl’s do-operator: externally set a variable), L3 asks what would have happened in one observed case.

✓ What L1 licenses

“Sentiment is linearly decodable from layer 12.”

✗ What L1 does not license

“The model represents sentiment at layer 12” or “Layer 12 computes sentiment.”

Decodability ≠ encoding; the information may be present but unused by the model’s own computation.

✓ What L2 licenses

“Intervening on head 9.6 changes IOI behavior under these tested conditions.”

✗ What L2 does not license

“Head 9.6 is the IOI mechanism” (sufficiency ≠ necessity; other paths may exist) or “This finding will hold on all IOI-like prompts” (requires transportability testing).

Transportability: whether a causal finding established on one prompt distribution carries over to another.

Diagnostic question · L3 Is the query anchored to a specific observation?

The Eiffel Tower counterfactual: abduction → action → prediction

Observed run: “The Eiffel Tower is in” → “Paris.” The counterfactual question: what would the model have output if a(5) had taken the value it takes when processing “The Colosseum is in”? The recipe is Pearl’s (2009) abduction–action–prediction.

(i)Abduction

Condition on the observed run—input, output, full trace—to infer the exogenous factors behind this particular forward pass. In the paper’s words: what about the model’s internal state, beyond the activations we recorded, determined that “The Eiffel Tower is in” → “Paris”?

(ii)Action

Intervene: set a(5) ← a′(5), the activation from the Colosseum prompt.

(iii)Prediction

Propagate forward, holding fixed the exogenous factors inferred in step (i).

U exogenous factors: not yet inferred
This run “The Eiffel Tower is in”→ …→ a(5)→ …→ “Paris”

Step (i) of the recipe is up next.

Where standard patching starts. Standard activation patching performs step (ii) only: it never infers what made this run produce “Paris,” so the prediction step has nothing to hold fixed. The patched output answers the interventional question—“does changing a(5) change the output?”—which is L2 evidence, even when it is narrated counterfactually. In general the interventional answer and the true counterfactual can diverge; here both happen to read “Rome,” so only the recipe—not the output—tells you which question was answered.

Tutorial illustration of the paper’s worked example; not a paper experiment.

✓ What L3 licenses

“For this specific forward pass, had a(5) taken the Colosseum value, the model would have output ‘Rome.’”

Not earned by this run: abduction was skipped, so the patched output is L2 evidence narrated counterfactually. Switch standard-patching mode off and run all three steps.

✗ What L3 does not license

Generalisations to other inputs (requires separate analysis or transportability assumptions) or mechanism-level claims like “layer 5 encodes location.”

L3 requires a structural causal model of how latent variables interact—an explicit model of how each internal variable is computed from its inputs and unobserved factors—not merely the ability to intervene.

What this shows

Each rung of Pearl’s causal ladder answers a different question, and the hierarchy is cumulative upward: associational evidence does not license interventional claims, and interventional evidence does not license counterfactual claims (Pearl, 2009; Bareinboim et al., 2022). The three scenes re-create the paper’s worked examples. The probe establishes that sentiment is decodable by an external classifier—the model is only observed, so the evidence stays on L1 no matter how causal the surrounding language sounds. The patch establishes that head 9.6 causally contributes under the tested conditions—an externally controlled manipulation, hence L2, but sufficiency under one intervention is not the mechanism.

The L3 stepper makes the final gap mechanical. A counterfactual is anchored to one observed forward pass, and answering it takes all three steps of the abduction–action–prediction recipe. Standard activation patching performs only the action step: nothing is inferred about what made this run produce “Paris,” so nothing can be held fixed when propagating forward. The result is interventional evidence narrated counterfactually—rung-elevated language, and the most consequential mismatch the paper diagnoses.

What the paper says

“Decodability ≠ encoding; the information may be present but unused by the model’s own computation.” — Appendix, “Locating an Interpretability Method on the Causal Ladder” (L1 example)
“Standard activation patching performs step (ii) but lacks the structural model needed for steps (i) and (iii). This is why most ‘counterfactual’ claims in interpretability are actually L2 claims narrated counterfactually.” — Appendix, “Locating an Interpretability Method on the Causal Ladder” (L3 example)

Try this

  1. Take an interpretability claim you know well—“feature X mediates behaviour Y”—and ask the three diagnostic questions in order. Which rung does the evidence occupy, and which rung does the language assert?
  2. In the L3 scene, switch on standard-patching mode and run the stepper again. State exactly what the skipped abduction step would need to infer for the Eiffel-Tower run, and why forward passes alone cannot supply it.
  3. With a partner: one of you defends “head 9.6 is the IOI mechanism,” the other may answer only with the L2 verdict cards. Then agree on the experiment that would strengthen the claim (necessity tests, transportability tests, or both).
