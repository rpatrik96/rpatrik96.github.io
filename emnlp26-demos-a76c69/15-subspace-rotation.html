Subspace ≠ Coordinates

A model’s truthful-vs-deceptive behaviour lives on a two-dimensional plane inside its representation space. Spin the basis within that plane: every behavioural readout (a fixed linear probe) is frozen while the axis labels go around, until an axis-identifying assumption pins them.

What this shows

Both green readouts use only the projection ΠS(h) — predicting the behaviour, or ablating the whole plane — and both are computed live from the toy points, yet neither budges at any φ. That is the mismatch: the evidence identifies the plane S, because it distinguishes S from other subspaces (remove it and the readout collapses to chance; any superspace of S passes that test too, so read S as the smallest subspace consistent with the evidence), but it places no constraint on where the axes sit inside S. Since ΠS(h) is the same object in every basis, a semantic label for an individual direction — “axis 1 is honesty” — admits a stronger reading than the subspace-level evidence supports: the same claim could be attached to any rotated pair of axes.

The coordinate-level readouts make the arbitrariness concrete. “Ablate axis 1 only” is a genuine intervention, yet its outcome depends on φ: at 30° it wipes the behaviour out, at 120° it does nothing, and both angles describe the same model and the same plane. Pinning the axes requires information beyond the behavioural evidence — an axis-identifying assumption. The toggle adds the paper’s example, sparsity, the same inductive bias sparse autoencoders and dictionary-learning methods impose on their learned basis: in this toy the in-plane coordinates hug two directions, so demanding sparse coordinates selects that basis up to permutation and sign (the assumption still accepts swapping the two axes or flipping their signs). The labels stop spinning because the assumption fixed them, not the behaviour — granted that the sparsity assumption actually holds for the representation.

What the paper says

“The method identifies a subspace S invariant under rotations within it, and not a particular basis.” — Mismatch box “Subspace ≠ Coordinates”, appendix taxonomy for characterising interpretability claims
“The subspace is identifiable; the axes within it are not.” — §“PCA: Subspace Structure ≠ Axis Semantics”

Try this

  1. Set φ = 30° and defend the claim “axis 1 encodes honesty” using only the right-hand readouts. Then set φ = 120° and defend “axis 1 is irrelevant to honesty.” Both defences cite real measurements; what changed between them, and what did not?
  2. “Ablate axis 1” is a genuine intervention, yet its outcome moves with φ, while “ablate the whole plane” does not. State precisely which object the interventional evidence identifies, and which extra choice the per-axis version smuggles in.
  3. Turn on the sparsity assumption and move φ to 90°: the assumption accepts this basis too. What freedom remains — which bases does the assumption still accept (its indeterminacy class) — and why does that residual freedom still leave per-axis semantics meaningful in a way the 45° blend would not?
