Observe or intervene? A confounder sandbox

Two hidden worlds produce the same scatter between a feature and a behaviour. Watching cannot separate them; setting the feature can.

Toy simulation: both worlds are sampled in your browser with a seeded PRNG, so every load is identical. Their parameters are chosen so the observational joint of (A, B) is the same bivariate Gaussian — zero mean, unit variances, correlation 0.8 — in both worlds; only the structural equations differ. The interventional columns use antithetic pairs, so their sample means sit exactly on the population value. What this shows

The two panels run different structural causal models with identical observational behaviour. In World I the feature causes the behaviour: B := 0.8·A + noise. In World II it never does: a hidden common cause C feeds both A and B. The parameters are matched so both worlds produce the same joint distribution, which means no statistic computed from observed samples — correlation, regression slope, the conditional mean E[B | A = a] — can tell them apart. More observational data sharpens the estimate of r; it never separates the worlds. On Pearl’s causal ladder (Pearl, 2009), correlation lives on rung one (seeing) and interventions on rung two (doing); a third rung, counterfactuals, lies beyond this demo’s scope. The interventional estimand — the quantity we are trying to pin down, p(B | do(A = a)) — is not identifiable from rung-one evidence: it cannot be computed from the observational distribution, however precisely we measure it.

The intervention slider changes the rung, not the sample size. Pinning A by do(A = a) severs A from whatever caused it upstream: in World I the outcome column tracks the dashed correlation-based forecast, while in World II it stays at baseline because the association ran through C all along. Read A as a probe direction or feature activation and B as a behaviour such as refusal, and this is the paper’s point about decoding evidence: the same L1 observations are equally consistent with the feature causing the behaviour and with an upstream concept in earlier layers producing both.

What the paper says

“For instance, observing that A correlates with B is consistent with A causing B, but equally consistent with a confounder causing both. Interventional evidence can distinguish these cases; associational evidence cannot.”

“Suppose we only observe that h(l) accurately predicts refusal. The estimand is not identifiable from the evidence we have, i.e., the available evidence could be equally well explained by earlier layers encoding an alternate concept that affects both the activations h(l) and refusal.”

— Causality is Key for Interpretability Claims to Generalise (arXiv:2602.16698), on identifiability

Try this

  1. Stay in Observe and try to decide which world hides the confounder, using any feature of the scatters you like. Then explain to your neighbour why no such feature can exist: the joints match by construction, so every observational statistic must agree.
  2. Switch to Intervene and drag do(A) to ±2. The dashed L1 forecast is the same in both panels; the gold interventional mean is not. Write the one-sentence claim each world now licenses, then press “Reveal the structures” to check yourself.
  3. Map it onto your own work: A is your probe direction or SAE feature, B the behaviour you care about. What plays C? Name one upstream concept that could produce both, and the activation-level intervention that would rule it out.
