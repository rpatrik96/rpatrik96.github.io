Pearl’s ladder of causation, one line: Rung 1 = observe (correlations in what the model shows) · Rung 2 = intervene (change something, watch the effect) · Rung 3 = counterfactual (what would this same input have done otherwise?). Every “rung” on this page refers to this ladder.
0 · Pick a claim card — the worksheet below follows your pick
1 · Estimand — “I am measuring …”
Estimand = the target quantity your experiment is meant to pin down (the estimator is the procedure that measures it). Free text, not graded — the debrief shows a sample answer to compare.
2 · My method provides evidence at rung:
Checklist hint: probing → observational; activation patching → interventional; interchange interventions (swap activations between two runs and test whether the outputs swap too) → counterfactual.
Drag the evidence strength: stronger numbers never move the method up a rung — only a different experiment does. Watch the what-if line in the claim box above.
3 · My claim requires evidence at rung:
Two ladders, two namings: methods are named by what you did (observe / intervene / counterfactual); claims by what your words assert (association / causation / mechanism — the paper’s codebook names). A mechanistic claim needs counterfactual-grade evidence.
Show the codebook’s linguistic markers per rung
Rung 1: “correlates with,” “is associated with,” “predicts,” “co-occurs with,” “information is present,” “is decodable from,” “can be extracted,” “activates on,” “fires when”
Rung 2: “causally affects,” “has causal effect on,” “mediates,” “influences,” “is sufficient for,” “can produce,” “enables,” “intervening on X changes Y,” “ablating X degrades Y”
Rung 3: “encodes,” “represents,” “computes,” “performs,” “THE circuit / mechanism / feature,” “controls,” “is responsible for,” “underlies,” “this head DOES X,” “uses X to do Y”
Codebook decision rule: hedges (“may encode,” “suggests that”) do not lower the rung — hedged claims are coded at the underlying claim’s rung, with the hedge reflected only in confidence.
4 · Rung check — method rung ≥ claim rung?
Pick a method rung and a claim rung to run the rung check.

What this shows

This is the tutorial’s live exercise: the paper’s practitioners’ checklist applied to concrete claims. Every diagnosis reduces to the same three moves — name the estimand, place the method on the ladder, place the claim’s language on the ladder — and then a single comparison: if the claim’s rung exceeds the method’s, the wording admits a stronger causal reading than the evidence licenses, and the checklist offers two repairs: strengthen the method (add interventions) or weaken the claim. The evidence-strength slider makes the orthogonality explicit: sweep the headline number from weak to near-perfect and the method’s rung never moves — only a different experiment moves it.

The bank pairs calibrated cases with rung-elevated ones. The IOI card is the paper’s own worked example of what interventional evidence licenses; the SAE-evaluation card is a documented calibration anchor whose verbs describe what the method does rather than what the model does internally. The six synthetic cards each exhibit one of the six recurring rung-elevation patterns from the paper’s annotation codebook — probing → “encodes”, SAE → “represents”, attention → “performs”, patching → “THE circuit”, steering → “controls”, ablation → “necessary”.

What the paper says

“Rung check: Method rung ≥ Claim rung? … If No: either strengthen method (add interventions) or weaken claim (‘correlates with’ instead of ‘implements’).”

“This checklist is not meant to discourage exploratory work or to raise publication barriers. A paper that states its rung, names its equivalence class, and bounds its scope contributes more than one whose language implies stronger evidence than reported — even if the former’s claims are narrower.”

— Causality is Key for Interpretability Claims to Generalise (arXiv:2602.16698), practitioners’ checklist

Term gloss (added for this exercise): an equivalence class is the set of alternative explanations — e.g., candidate circuits — that the reported evidence cannot tell apart.

Try this

  1. Start the Solo 2:00 timer, pick one card, and commit: write the estimand in your own words and select both rungs before revealing the debrief. The estimand is the hardest box on the checklist — if you cannot fill it, that is itself a diagnosis. Then drag the evidence-strength slider to both ends: does your rung call change? (It should not.)
  2. Start Pair 3:00 and compare with your neighbour on a card where you disagreed. The codebook’s edge rules usually settle it: hedges do not lower a claim’s rung, and each claim is coded against the method that directly supports it.
  3. Run your own most recent abstract through steps 1–4. If the rung check fails, decide which repair you would actually ship: the weakened sentence, or the added intervention.
