Setup
Two synthetic models perform the same binary task, and a concept c is present somewhere in each 8-dimensional hidden state h. Think of c as a POS-like feature in an LLM’s residual stream and the task as the model’s next-token decision. Start by training a linear probe for c on each model.
What the probe licensed · L1, rung 1 of Pearl’s ladder (association: what co-occurs)
What the intervention licensed · L2, rung 2 (intervention: what happens when we act)
Pearl’s ladder ranks evidence: rung 1 is association (what co-occurs), rung 2 is intervention (what happens when we act on the system). Reporting “the model encodes c” on probe evidence alone is rung-elevated language: the phrasing admits a causal reading (the model uses c) that the associational estimand does not license. The rung-appropriate claim is “c is linearly decodable from h”.

Toy simulation, seeded and deterministic: two 8-D Gaussian “models” with linear readouts; the probe is a difference-of-class-means direction; the ablation mean-projects that direction out of h. Built for this tutorial as an illustration; it is not an experiment from the paper.

What this shows

Both models carry concept c in their hidden state, and a linear probe decodes it from either one at roughly 97–98% held-out accuracy. The probe view of the two models is indistinguishable, yet their causal structure differs: Model B’s output readout consumes the c-direction, whereas in Model A the same direction is a bystander correlate that the data injected and the computation ignores. A probe targets an associational estimand (is the information present?), so it cannot separate these two worlds.

The ablation asks the intervention-effect question: remove the direction the probe found, then watch behaviour. Task accuracy falls to chance only in Model B, while a fresh probe on the ablated states sits near chance in both models (≈51% / ≈53%), so no linear probe can recover c from either model; only the behavioural consequence differs. This is the logic of amnesic probing (Elazar et al., 2021) behind the paper’s BERT example: probes decode part-of-speech tags from BERT at >97% accuracy (Hewitt & Manning, 2019), yet removing that information barely affects language modelling. Decodable, and still unused.

What the paper says

“Probing shows c is decodable from h but claiming it is encoded by the model requires knowing that the model uses it.”

— arXiv:2602.16698, “Decodability ≠ Model Use” mismatch box (Takeaway)

“A linear probe trained on BERT’s hidden states achieves >97% accuracy at predicting POS tags (Hewitt & Manning, 2019). […] More strikingly, Elazar et al. (2021) demonstrated that removing the information exploited by POS probes via causal intervention (‘amnesic probing’) had minimal effect on BERT’s language modeling performance”

— arXiv:2602.16698, worked example on BERT and part-of-speech

Try this

  1. Train the probes, then stop. Write down which model you believe uses c, with a confidence. What in the probe panel could justify anything other than a coin flip?
  2. After the ablation, compare the two fresh-probe accuracies: both sit near chance (≈51% / ≈53%), so no linear probe can recover c from either model, and only behaviour separates them. Then lower the ablation strength: Model B’s task accuracy degrades smoothly, yet the fresh probes stay near 97–98% until full strength, because shrinking a direction is invertible and the information survives. Which claims in your favourite probing paper would survive this test?
  3. Rewrite “Model A encodes c” at the rung the evidence supports, then name the experiment that would license the original verb. Control tasks (Hewitt & Liang, 2019) guard against a different probing failure, namely probe memorisation: which panel of this demo would they change?
