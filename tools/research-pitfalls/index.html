Research Pitfalls · The Study Game

Set up a study. Try not to fall in.

A game for the first year of a PhD. You make the thirteen decisions a study is made of; each one names the pitfall it risks, what it costs, and the fix. Then you judge other people's plans and see whether your confidence matches your accuracy: that is taste, and it is trainable.

A disclaimer: the pitfalls are well documented, but which option is “sound” and which plan is “stronger” is partly one researcher's judgment. Where the game disagrees with you, the reveal gives its reasons; you are allowed to disagree back.

Your domain

The pitfalls are universal; the wording isn't. Machine learning is the main focus; the other domains reword the same traps and add a few of their own.

Runs in your browser; nothing is sent anywhere. Progress stays on this device. Dotted terms have a one-line explanation on hover; every card also lists its terms underneath, tap-friendly.

        How this is scored

        What is being scored. Agreement with the game's answers, which are one researcher's judgment of what a careful reviewer would say. The principles behind them are documented in each reveal; where your reasoning is sound and the game's pick is not, the disagreement is the useful part.

        Points. Right: sure +4, leaning +3, guess +1. Wrong: sure −6, leaning −2, guess +1. The table is built so that the point-maximizing choice is the honest one: “leaning” pays more than “guess” once you believe above 60%, and “sure” pays more than “leaning” above 80%, the midpoints between the stated 50 / 70 / 90. Sure-and-wrong is the expensive case, as it is in review.

        Calibration. A forecaster who says “70%” should be right on 70% of those days. For each confidence level, your stated probability (which is lower for four-option items, where a guess is 25%) is compared with how often you were right at that level; the weighted average of the gaps is the calibration error, the same quantity as expected calibration error for a classifier. The over/under verdict uses the signed gap.

        Level. Points count toward your level only the first time you see an item, so repeating a round does not level you up. A flawless, all-sure round is worth 40 points; the top level needs 60, so it takes more than one round, and in practice more than one domain.

        The answers are opinionated: one reviewer's taste, argued in each reveal. Disagree if you can say why; that is also how taste gets built.
