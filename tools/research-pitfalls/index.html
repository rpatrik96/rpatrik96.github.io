Set up a study. Try not to fall in.

A text-only game for the first year of a PhD. You make the decisions a study actually consists of — question, design, execution, analysis, reporting — and the game tells you which classic pitfall you just walked into, why it was tempting, and what to read. A second part trains taste: the skill of telling a strong plan from a weak one before anyone has run it.

Your domain

Runs in your browser; nothing is sent anywhere. Progress is kept on this device. It is a game, not a checklist: the point is the argument you have with yourself after each reveal.

      How this is scored

      Taste points. Right and sure +3, right and leaning +2, right on a coin flip +1; wrong and sure −3, wrong and leaning −1, wrong on a coin flip 0. Being sure and wrong is the expensive case — as it is in review.

      Calibration. Your stated confidence (coin flip 50%, leaning 70%, sure 90%) is compared with how often you were actually right. A weather forecaster who says “70%” should be right on 70% of those days. If your average confidence is well above your accuracy you are overconfident; well below, underconfident. This is the same idea as expected calibration error for a classifier, with three bins.

      Level. Cumulative taste points across rounds on this device. It resets with your progress.

      The answers are opinionated: they encode one reviewer's taste, argued in the reveal. Disagreeing with a reason is allowed — that is also how taste gets built.
