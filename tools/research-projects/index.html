Research Projects · The Management Game

Run a research project without dropping the ball

A game for the year you first lead a project: a supervisor with no time, a senior collaborator with many projects, a junior you are responsible for, and a deadline. Every decision moves six hidden dials; the classic problems (time pressure, authorship fights, silent collaborators, dropped balls, burnout) fire when the dials say so, and most can be prevented or repaired. The debrief traces every problem back to the decision that seeded it.

A disclaimer: the situations are drawn from real lab-culture discussions and from the Path to PhD newsletter, but which move is “sound” is one researcher's judgment, and every reveal gives its reasons.

Before month 1

Your situation

You are a second-year PhD student, and for the first time the project is yours to run. The idea came out of a workshop conversation: a method your lab has been circling for a while, with a theory part and an experimental part. Your supervisor likes it, a professor at another institute wants in, and the target is a conference deadline in month 8. Nobody has written down who does what.

Nine months, two or three decisions a month. Each decision moves six dials you cannot see directly (alignment, trust, buffer, credit, energy, feedback loop), and the classic problems fire when the dials say so: a collaborator goes quiet, an authorship question surfaces late, a deadline lands on a week with no slack. Most can be prevented, and most can be repaired if you notice in time. At the end, a debrief traces every problem back to the decision that seeded it.

Four people matter, and they are introduced below with what they are to you. Their personalities are drawn fresh each run, and they change which move is sound: a supervisor who wants to be asked and one who trusts you to decide need different messages; a student who hides being stuck needs a different kind of attention from one who asks. When a decision depends on the person, the reveal says so, and says what would change with the other kind.

Three things to do differently

Reading list from this run
    How this is scored

    Six dials, 0 to 100. Alignment (does everyone share the goal, the milestones and the expectations), trust (how safe it is to say hard things in this team), buffer (slack between now and the deadline), credit (how clear roles and authorship are), energy (yours), and loop (how quickly you find out when something is off). Each option moves some of them; the reveal shows which.

    Problems. Each problem in the field manual has triggers: choices that raise its odds and choices that lower them. At its month the game rolls against those odds; a problem that would have fired at high odds but did not, because of something you did, is logged as prevented. A few problems can fire at low odds whatever you do, because some cannot be avoided, only handled.

    Outcome. Submitted on time needs buffer above zero and enough progress; the quality of the paper follows progress and loop; team health follows trust and alignment; sustainability follows energy. The verdict weighs the submission (30 points), then quality, team health, your energy and credit clarity, so a paper shipped over a broken team or an empty tank does not read as a success.

    People. Each run gives the four characters one of two personalities. On a few cards the personality changes which option is sound (a supervisor who trusts you to decide versus one who wants to be asked), and on more of them it changes the reason; the reveal names the dependence and what the other kind of person would need. Some problems are also more or less likely with some people.

    Profile. Each option carries a lean on one of five spectra; the profile counts where your picks sat. Neither pole is the goal.

    Subjective. Which option is sound is one researcher's judgment, grounded in the readings in the field manual. The reveal always gives the reason.

    Your messages, scored

    Most management problems are communication problems. The difference between the two messages was rarely tone. It was whether the message carried context, a concrete ask, a date, and a way for the other person to say no.
